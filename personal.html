Personal Project Pitch

When a student pastes a document into an AI slide generation tool, and the AI misses the key content they need, how best can that be corrected early in the process? Without such a panel, the user may not realize what content is missing until they are presenting/ may never realize it at all.
A Content lineage panel would show the user which what points where taken from the original document and what is missing.

Intended user group

This project is aimed at people, particularly students who use an AI tool such as PowerPoint Copilot or Gamma to turn a longer document or outline into a slide deck. This covers students presenting a report, turning a proposal into a pitch deck, and hands a document to an AI and gets slides back in return.

Observation of real users

I watched a classmate generate a slide deck from a project report using an AI slide tool. The deck looked clean and well organized, but once she started rehearsing, she kept finding gaps. A figure from the report was missing from the slides entirely, and a limitation she had written a full paragraph about had been shrunk down to a single bullet point. She only noticed this while rehearsing out loud, not while reading the deck on screen. This made it difficult to identify which pages were important which had been omitted. This is the problem this project is aimed at solving.

Literature review

The generation side of this problem already has research behind it. Sun and colleagues frame document to slide generation as a summarization task, where a model has to choose which content from a longer document earns a place on a slide and which gets left out (Sun et al., 2021). Fu and colleagues build this into a fuller pipeline that adds retrieval and layout on top of summarization, and give a concrete sense of how much of a source document actually survives into a generated deck (Fu et al., 2022). Together these two papers describe the exact choice point my project is meant to make visible to the presenter, but neither studies what happens once a person has to actually stand up and present the result, which is the gap this project sits in.

The trust and verification side points more directly at my actual research question. A study of AI generated summaries in mobile reading found a real gap between how much people trust a summary and how well they understand the source material after reading only the summary, and the authors recommend labeling AI provenance and offering verification prompts when accuracy matters, which is close to what a content lineage panel is meant to do (Fan, Liu, and Huang, 2025). At the same time, a study of how people actually use references offered alongside AI generated answers found that engagement stayed low across nine different systems, with people generally trusting an answer that sounded plausible over checking the source behind it (Ouyang and Narechania, 2026). That second finding is a real risk worth planning for, since adding a lineage panel does not guarantee anyone opens it. My own small study would possibly measure whether people actually look at the panel before a presentation, not only whether the panel is accurate when they do.

What is the Goal?

To create an interface layer on top of a normal document to slides pipeline. A minimal version can be built with a basic web app. The user pastes in a document, an LLM API call generates slide content the way these tools already do, and alongside each slide a small panel lists which section of the source document it came from and a short note on what was condensed or left out.

Design

The panel sits alongside the generated deck rather than inside a single slide, since burying it in the slides themselves would defeat the point. The current plan is a simple list, one entry per omitted or condensed point, each with a short title, the page of the source document it came from, and a one line note on whether it was dropped entirely or just shrunk down. Clicking an entry expands it to show the original wording from the source document, so the student can decide on the spot whether it is worth mentioning out loud or safe to leave out. The visual treatment stays plain on purpose, a bordered box with clear headings, since the goal is something a student actually reads in the five minutes before a presentation, not a feature they have to learn.

Methodology

The evaluation is with students who have used an AI tool to generate slides before. Each participant brings or is given a short document and its AI generated deck, with a few points deliberately dropped or condensed by the generation step. Half the participants rehearse their presentation with the lineage panel available, half without, then everyone switches to the other condition with a second document, so order effects can be controlled for. During rehearsal, a researcher plays the role of an audience member and asks one question tied directly to an omitted point. The main measure is whether the student can answer that question well, either because the panel helped them catch the gap beforehand or because they happened to know the material anyway, along with whether they opened the panel at all when it was available. That second measure matters because of what the literature review found, a feature being accurate does not mean anyone uses it, so this study needs to check actual use, not just accuracy, before claiming the panel helps.

Skills needed

  • Basic web development, HTML, CSS, and JavaScript for the interface and the side panel
  • Using LLM API and writing prompts that return structured output, such as slide text plus a lineage note
  • Learning slide rendering approach, either a simple custom layout or an existing library
